## Stage 110: Antigua to Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado
**Saturday, November 29, 2025 | 142 km | 992m up, 2,474m down**

### Starting Point: Antigua - UNESCO World Heritage City

Antigua is Guatemala's crown jewel and one of the best-preserved colonial cities in the Americas. Founded on March 10, 1543, as the capital of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, the city served as the political, religious, and cultural center of Spanish Central America for over 200 years until the devastating 1773 earthquake prompted relocation of the capital to present-day Guatemala City.

The city sits at 1,500 meters elevation in a valley surrounded by three dramatic volcanoes: Agua (dormant), Fuego (highly active), and Acatenango. Its grid-pattern layout, inspired by Italian Renaissance urban planning principles, features cobblestone streets lined with colorful colonial buildings in shades of mustard yellow, terracotta, and burgundy. The architectural style, known as Barroco Antigueño, uses thick walls and low bell towers designed to withstand earthquakes.

UNESCO designated Antigua as a World Heritage Site in 1979 for its outstanding Spanish Baroque architecture and its importance during the colonial period. The city is filled with magnificent churches, convents, and civic buildings, many preserved as romantic ruins with bougainvillea-draped walls open to the sky.

#### Key Antigua Attractions (Pre-Departure)

**Parque Central (Central Plaza)**  
The heart of Antigua, dominated by an ornate fountain featuring sirens based on Mayan folklore. Surrounded by significant buildings including the Cathedral, Palace of the Captains General, and municipal offices. Perfect spot for early morning coffee before your ride.

**Arco de Santa Catalina**  
Antigua's most photographed landmark, this iconic yellow arch with a clock tower was built in the 17th century to allow nuns to pass between convent buildings without being seen on the street. Located on 5a Avenida Norte.

**Catedral de Santiago**  
The cathedral's white baroque façade faces Parque Central. Built after the 1773 earthquake, it fronts massive ruins of the original 16th-century cathedral. The ruins behind (separate entrance, ~40 Quetzales) reveal the scale of the original structure.

**Convento de Las Capuchinas**  
One of Antigua's most intact ruins, featuring a unique circular tower with tiny nun's cells arranged around a central patio. The architecture blends Spanish colonial, baroque, and Moorish styles. The retreat tower offers fascinating insight into convent life.

**Casa Santo Domingo Museum Complex**  
A luxury hotel built within Santo Domingo monastery ruins, housing six museums including colonial art, pre-Columbian artifacts, glass, silver, and pharmacy collections. The preserved ruins and gardens are spectacular. Open to non-guests for museum entry.

### Route Highlights: Antigua to the Coast

#### Volcanic Landscape (Departure from Antigua)

As you leave Antigua heading south, the route provides spectacular views of the three volcanoes that frame the city. Volcán de Agua (3,760m) dominates the southern skyline, its perfect cone a dormant giant. To the west, Volcán de Fuego (3,763m), one of Central America's most active volcanoes, frequently sends ash plumes into the sky. Adjacent to Fuego stands Volcán Acatenango (3,976m), popular for overnight hiking expeditions to watch Fuego's eruptions.

#### Volcán de Pacaya Access Point

Approximately 30-40km into your ride, you'll pass near access points to Volcán de Pacaya, another of Guatemala's active volcanoes and one of the most popular hiking destinations in the country.

**Pacaya Volcano (2,552m)**  
One of three constantly active volcanoes in Guatemala, Pacaya has been erupting since 1965. The most recent significant eruption was in March 2021, creating fresh lava flows. The volcano offers relatively accessible hiking (2-3 hours round trip from the trailhead at San Francisco de Sales) with opportunities to see hardened lava fields, fumaroles, and volcanic landscapes. Tours from Antigua are popular and include marshmallow-toasting on heated volcanic rocks. While you won't have time for the hike during your ride, the volcano is visible from the route and represents the geologically active landscape you're traversing.

#### Escuintla Department - Tropical Transition

As you descend from the highlands, you'll transition through Guatemala's coffee-growing region on volcanic slopes before entering the hot, humid lowlands of Escuintla Department. This region is Guatemala's agricultural powerhouse, dominated by sugar cane, palm oil, and banana plantations. The department extends from the volcanic highlands to the black-sand beaches of the Pacific coast.

**Climate Note:** The descent brings dramatic temperature increases. Antigua sits at 1,500m with temperate highland climate, while the coastal plain is tropical, hot, and humid. Plan for early departure to avoid midday heat.

### Pacific Coastal Zone

#### Escuintla City (Possible Stop)

Guatemala's third-largest city (population ~130,000), Escuintla serves primarily as a commercial and transportation hub rather than a tourist destination. However, it offers practical amenities for cyclists:

- Large central market below the plaza with food options
- Restaurants serving fresh seafood and ceviche
- Refueling stations
- Banks and ATMs

The city sits at the junction of CA-9 highway (from Guatemala City) and roads heading to various Pacific coast destinations. The area is busy, hot, and focused on industrial and agricultural commerce. Most travelers use it as a brief stop rather than a destination.

#### Black Sand Beaches

Guatemala's Pacific coastline is characterized by volcanic black sand beaches, a result of the country's 37 volcanoes. The sand is dramatically dark, almost charcoal-colored, creating a stark contrast with the blue Pacific waters. The beaches along this coast are separated from the mainland by the Canal de Chiquimulilla, a narrow waterway running parallel to the coast.

**Swimming Caution:** The Pacific coast here has strong currents and undertows. These beaches are better known for surfing than swimming.

### Approaching the Border

#### Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado

Your destination for Stage 110 is this small border town (population ~2,800) on the Guatemalan side of the Río Paz, which forms the international border with El Salvador. The town is named after Spanish conquistador Pedro de Alvarado, who built ships here that he used to sail to Peru.

The town sits on Highway CA-2, which runs along Guatemala's Pacific coast. It's 15km from the Pacific Ocean and handles significant commercial traffic between Guatemala and El Salvador. While not a tourist destination, it's an important crossing point for trade and travel.

**Border Crossing Note:** The Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado / La Hachadura crossing is one of three main Guatemala-El Salvador border points. It's the southernmost crossing, closest to the Pacific coast, and handles the highest volume of cargo by weight, though it has fewer passenger crossings than the CA-1 (Pan-American Highway) route.

**Accommodation:** Limited options in Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado itself. Most travelers either continue to El Salvador or return to larger Guatemalan cities. For tour purposes, accommodation will be pre-arranged.

**Stage 110 Summary:**  
This is a dramatic stage, beginning in colonial grandeur at 1,500m elevation and descending through volcanic landscapes, coffee plantations, and tropical lowlands to reach the Pacific coastal plain. The 2,474m descent makes this one of the longest downhill days of the tour. The heat and humidity increase significantly as you lose elevation. Early departure from Antigua is strongly recommended.

## Stage 111: Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado to El Tunco
**Sunday, November 30, 2025 | 120 km | 1,293m up, 1,328m down**

### Guatemala-El Salvador Border Crossing

#### La Hachadura / Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado Border

You'll begin the day by crossing the international border between Guatemala and El Salvador. The Guatemalan side is Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado; the Salvadoran side is La Hachadura. The border is formed by the Río Paz, crossed by Puente La Hachadura (La Hachadura Bridge).

**Bridge Infrastructure:** A new bridge (Puente Manuel José Arce) was completed in 2024 to replace the narrow older bridge. The new structure is 160m long with 4 lanes and improved access roads, significantly improving traffic flow. Construction began in November 2021 with a $30 million investment.

**Border Crossing Process for Cyclists:**

*Leaving Guatemala:*
- Present passport at Guatemalan immigration for exit stamp (no fee)
- No fruit or vegetables allowed when leaving Guatemala

*Entering El Salvador:*
- Present passport with at least 6 months validity
- Purchase tourist card ($10 USD)
- Proof of onward travel may be requested
- Tourist cards typically valid for 90 days within the CA-4 region (Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua)

**Tour Support Note:** Your tour support team will assist with the border crossing process, which typically takes 1-2 hours for groups. Have your passport, cash (US dollars accepted), and patience ready.

**Traffic Note:** This border can experience heavy truck traffic, particularly after disruptions. Long lines of commercial vehicles are common. Cyclists and light vehicles can often bypass truck queues.

### Entering El Salvador

El Salvador is Central America's smallest country by area but has made significant tourism investments in recent years. The country has dramatically improved security since 2019, when a new president initiated major law enforcement changes that have made El Salvador much safer for tourists. The Pacific coast region, particularly the "Surf City" initiative around El Tunco and La Libertad, is a major focus of tourism development.

**Language:** Spanish predominates. English is limited even in tourist areas, though more common in surf towns like El Tunco.

**Currency:** US Dollar (USD) is El Salvador's official currency since 2001, making transactions easy for North American travelers.

**Safety:** La Libertad Department and coastal areas are considered safe for tourists. El Salvador has invested heavily in coastal revitalization and maintaining tourist-friendly zones.

### Coastal Route Through El Salvador

The route follows Highway CA-2 (Carretera del Litoral / Coastal Highway) eastward through La Libertad Department, El Salvador's Pacific coastal region.

#### Pacific Slope and Coffee Country

As with Guatemala's Pacific coast, you'll be riding through a landscape shaped by volcanic activity. The volcanic soil makes this region ideal for agriculture, particularly coffee cultivation on the slopes above the coastal plain.

#### La Libertad City (En Route)

Approximately midway through the stage, you'll pass through or near La Libertad city, the capital of La Libertad Department and El Salvador's surf capital.

**Puerto de La Libertad**  
El Salvador's first port, established for commercial trade in 1831. The first steamboat arrived in 1857. While newer ports (La Unión and Acajutla) have eclipsed it economically, the historic pier remains a vibrant attraction. The massive pier uses a crane to lower fishing boats into the ocean. Local fishermen sell fresh catches including snapper, tuna, rays, squid, shrimp, lobster, blue crabs (jaibas), clams, and scallops.

**Malecón (Seaside Promenade)**  
In recent years, El Salvador has invested in revitalizing La Libertad's waterfront. The malecón is a paved, landscaped promenade stretching from the pier to Playa La Paz, featuring benches, viewpoints, restaurants, and ice cream shops. It's become a popular destination for Salvadorans from San Salvador (30 minutes away) and represents the government's successful efforts to reclaim public spaces.

**Punta Roca**  
La Libertad's famous surf break, considered one of the best right-hand point breaks in Central America. The main break extends about 800m from La Punta to the town, with three main sections:
- La Punta: Fast, hollow waves at the top of the point
- Cemetery section: Middle break
- La Paz: Inside cove section, good for longboarding

Punta Roca hosts regional and international surf championships. The wave can produce rides of 300m or more on good days with 6-8 foot swells. Floodlights installed by the city allow dramatic nighttime views of the break.

### Destination: El Tunco

Your stage ends at Playa El Tunco, El Salvador's most famous surf beach and the heart of the country's "Surf City" tourism initiative.

**El Tunco Overview:**  
A tiny beach village that has become El Salvador's premier surf destination and most popular tourist spot. The town consists essentially of two main streets packed with surf shops, hostels, restaurants, beach bars, and surf schools. Despite rapid growth, it maintains a laid-back, authentic surf town vibe without major commercialization.

The name "El Tunco" comes from a pig-shaped rock formation visible from the beach (tunco is Salvadoran slang for pig).

**Location:** Approximately 40km from San Salvador International Airport, 45 minutes by car. Part of La Libertad Department's coastal Surf City zone.

**Town Character:**  
Very small, walkable in 5 minutes, with guarded gates at entry points providing security. Most action centers on the beach and the pedestrian street at the west end of town. International surf community mixing with local Salvadorans creates cosmopolitan yet relaxed atmosphere.

**Beach:**  
Black volcanic sand beach, separated from the village by a small rocky area. The famous pig-shaped rock sits in the middle of the beach. At low tide, the beach reveals rock pools. The beach itself can be rocky with driftwood, part of its rugged charm. Strong surf and currents make it primarily a surf beach rather than a swimming beach.

**Surf Breaks Near El Tunco:**

*La Bocana* - El Tunco's most powerful wave, a fast left-hand break at the river mouth. Hollow, punchy sections for advanced surfers. Works best on solid south/southwest swells with north offshores.

*El Sunzal* - Just a short walk north of El Tunco, this is the area's most famous wave. A long, mellow right-hand point break offering extended rides perfect for intermediates and longboarders. Multiple sections provide opportunities for all levels. One of the most consistent waves in El Salvador.

*Sunzalito* - Beach break between El Sunzal and El Tunco, good for beginners with sandy bottom and softer waves.

*La Bocanita* - Beach break between La Bocana and El Tunco, workable on higher tides.

**Accommodation Range:** From budget hostels with dorm beds (~$20/night) to beachfront hotels and private rooms ($50+ per night). Many properties have ocean views and easy beach access.

**Stage 111 Summary:**  
This stage involves an international border crossing followed by coastal riding through El Salvador's Pacific lowlands. The route brings you to one of Central America's premier surf destinations, where you'll spend your rest day. The riding is less mountainous than Stage 110 but still features moderate climbing. Heat and humidity are significant factors in this tropical coastal zone.

## Rest Day: El Tunco, El Salvador
**Monday, December 1, 2025**

After two days and 262km of riding including a 2,500m descent from Antigua and an international border crossing, you've earned this rest day in one of Central America's most vibrant beach towns.

### Surfing - The Main Event

El Tunco is fundamentally a surf destination. Even if you've never surfed, this is an excellent place to learn, with numerous surf schools offering beginner lessons.

**Surf Lessons:** Professional instruction costs approximately $20-35 USD for 2-3 hours, including board rental and rashguard. Most schools offer:
- Beach instruction on fundamentals
- Guided water practice
- Equipment for the full day after your lesson
- Small class sizes or private instruction

**Board Rentals:** If you're already a surfer, board rentals cost approximately $15/day. Wetsuits aren't necessary as water temperature stays 26-29°C year-round.

**Conditions:** Late November/early December is in the dry season with generally favorable offshore morning winds. While the biggest swells come March-October (wet season), there's consistent surf year-round.

**Recommended Schools/Guides:**
- Bamba Surf Coach (Instagram: @bambasurfcoach) - Competitive surfer turned instructor, excellent English
- Local shops along the main street offer lessons and rentals

**Surf Etiquette:** El Sunzal and nearby breaks can get crowded. Respect local surfers, wait your turn, and if you're a beginner, your instructor will guide you to appropriate spots away from the main lineup.

### Beach Life

**Sunrise/Sunset Viewing:**  
El Tunco offers spectacular Pacific sunsets. The beach comes alive at golden hour with locals and tourists gathering to watch the sun sink into the ocean. Sunrise surfers catch the best conditions with offshore winds and fewer crowds.

**Beach Relaxation:**  
While the black sand gets extremely hot in midday sun and the beach can be rocky, it's perfect for lounging in the morning or late afternoon. Bring water shoes for comfort on hot sand and rocks.

**Ocean Pools:**  
At low tide, rock pools form along the shoreline, creating interesting tide pool exploration opportunities.

### Food & Drink

El Tunco has a surprisingly sophisticated food scene for such a small town, mixing international cuisine with authentic Salvadoran dishes.

**Must-Try: Pupusas**  
Pupusas are El Salvador's national dish - thick corn tortillas stuffed with cheese, beans, meat, or vegetables, served with curtido (pickled cabbage slaw) and salsa roja. They're delicious, cheap (~$1 per pupusa), and found everywhere.

**Recommended Pupuserías:**
- Pupuseria La Guanaquita - Wide variety, authentic
- Dale Dale Café - Regular menu includes pupusas
- Loroco Bistro - Creative fusion pupusas with international twists

**Seafood:**  
Fresh catches from local fishermen feature prominently. Try ceviche, grilled fish, and seafood cocktails. Prices typically $7-15 for substantial portions.

**International Options:**
- *Mopelia* - Tapas in garden setting with fairy lights, special prices 4-7pm
- *Jungle* - Mexican food including crispy deep-fried avocado tacos, margaritas, live music
- *Dale Dale Café* - Mix of local and international
- *Loroco Bistro* - Creative fusion cuisine

**Beer & Coffee:**
- *Cadejo Brewing Company* - Local El Salvador craft brewery, tours available
- *Multiple cafes* serving Salvadoran coffee throughout town

### Nightlife

El Tunco is known for vibrant nightlife, though it's entirely optional if you prefer early-to-bed surf schedules.

**Beach Bars & Clubs:**
- *La Guitarra Bar* - Live music venue, fun pub vibe right on the beach. Great for dancing. Note: if you like sleep, don't stay in the attached hotel
- *La Bonita Beach Club* - Boho-aesthetic beach club with stunning views, beautiful setting for cocktails. Premium prices but worth it for ambiance
- *Kako's GastroBar* - Good vibes, drinks with a view

**Atmosphere:**  
The town transforms after sunset, with bars and restaurants lighting up, music filling the streets, and a festive, social atmosphere. Nightlife peaks on weekends but is lively throughout the week. The scene is friendly, international, and ranges from casual beachfront lounging to dancing until late.

### Nearby Attractions & Day Trips

If you want to venture beyond El Tunco on your rest day:

**Tamanique Waterfalls**  
Scenic waterfalls accessible by half-day trip. Multiple cascades with swimming holes. Brings adventure hiking with refreshing rewards.

**Santa Ana Volcano (Ilamatepec)**  
El Salvador's highest volcano (2,381m). Last erupted in 2005. The summit crater contains a stunning turquoise sulfur lake. Hiking takes 4-5 hours round trip. Popular day trip from the coast, though it makes for a long rest day. Well worth it if you're not volcano'd-out after the Antigua region.

**Joya de Cerén Archaeological Site**  
UNESCO World Heritage Site known as the "Pompeii of the Americas." A Mayan village preserved in volcanic ash from an eruption around 600 AD. Provides remarkable insight into daily life of common Maya people. Located about 35km from El Tunco.

**El Zonte**  
Another surf beach about 15km west of El Tunco. Smaller, slightly more mellow than El Tunco. Good for a change of scenery if you want to explore more of the coast.

### Practical Information for Rest Day

**Safety:**  
El Tunco is very safe, with guarded entry gates and strong tourist police presence. Feel comfortable walking around day or night. However, use common sense - don't leave valuables on the beach, watch your belongings, and be aware of your surroundings.

**Getting Around:**
- El Tunco itself is walkable in 5 minutes
- Tuk-tuks available for short trips
- Buses to nearby beaches cost $1-2
- Organized tours available for volcano or waterfall trips

**Services:**
- ATMs available (though have USD cash for best rates)
- Most places accept credit cards
- WiFi widely available in cafes and accommodations
- Pharmacies and basic services in La Libertad (10 minutes away)

**Weather:**  
Late November/early December is dry season. Expect hot (28-32°C), humid conditions with low rainfall probability. Sunscreen, hat, and hydration essential.

**Recovery Focus:**  
Remember this is a rest day after significant mileage. Consider:
- Morning surf lesson (active recovery, different muscles)
- Massage (available from local practitioners)
- Beach time, swimming in ocean pools
- Light exploring and good food
- Early night to recover for continuing the tour

## Regional Context: Guatemala's Pacific Coast to El Salvador

### Geological Setting

This section of Central America sits along the Pacific Ring of Fire, resulting in dramatic volcanic landscapes and volcanic sand beaches. The route traverses the southern slopes of the Central American volcanic arc, where subduction of the Cocos Plate beneath the Caribbean Plate creates the chain of active volcanoes.

Guatemala has 37 volcanoes, including Fuego (one of the world's most active) and Pacaya. El Salvador has over 20 volcanic formations. This geology makes the soil exceptionally fertile for coffee and other crops but also creates earthquake and eruption hazards.

### Cultural Transition: Maya Guatemala to Spanish-influenced Salvador

**Guatemala (Highland to Coast):**  
The route begins in Antigua, heart of Spanish colonial power in Central America and a cultural capital for 230 years. The highlands have significant indigenous Maya populations (though Antigua itself is more Ladino/mestizo). Spanish colonial influence dominates architecture, religion, and culture.

Moving to the Pacific lowlands, the population becomes predominantly Ladino (mixed Spanish and indigenous ancestry) with culture focused on agriculture and commerce rather than highland Maya traditions.

**El Salvador:**  
El Salvador's indigenous population was largely assimilated during the colonial period and through 20th-century policies. The country is predominantly mestizo with strong Spanish cultural influence. The Pacific coast culture is relaxed, beach-oriented, and increasingly influenced by international surf tourism.

El Salvador has a complex recent history. A devastating civil war (1979-1992) and gang violence in the 2000s-2010s gave the country a reputation for danger. However, major security improvements since 2019 have transformed tourism potential. The coastal areas have long been safer than urban centers.

### Coffee Culture

Both Guatemala's Pacific slope and El Salvador's volcanic highlands are prime coffee-growing regions. The volcanic soil, altitude (800-1,600m for optimal growing), and climate create ideal conditions for high-quality Arabica coffee.

**Guatemalan coffee** from regions like Antigua and the southern slopes is renowned worldwide, with Antigua coffee considered among the finest. Fincas (plantations) dot the volcanic hillsides.

**El Salvador** has a strong coffee tradition, though less internationally recognized than Guatemala. Coffee cultivation shapes the landscape and economy of the volcanic slopes inland from the coast.

### The Pacific Coast Road (CA-2)

Highway CA-2 (Carretera al Pacífico / Coastal Highway) runs along Central America's Pacific coast through Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, and beyond. This is a major transportation corridor for commerce and tourism, connecting Pacific port cities and coastal communities. The road quality varies but is generally good in this section, as it handles significant truck traffic.

### Border Regions and CA-4 Agreement

The Guatemala-El Salvador border you'll cross is part of the CA-4 free movement area comprising Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, and Nicaragua. Citizens of these countries can move freely between them. Foreign tourists receive a single visa valid for 90 days across all four countries, with border checks focused on immigration stamps and security rather than detailed customs procedures for tourists.

### Surf Culture and Tourism Development

El Salvador has aggressively promoted "Surf City" as a national brand since 2015, marketing its Pacific coast as a world-class surf destination. Infrastructure investments, security improvements, and international competitions have raised the profile. The region hosted ISA World Masters Surf Championships and attracts surfers from around the world.

Guatemala's Pacific coast has developed more slowly for tourism, with Monterrico and El Paredon as the main beach destinations. Surfing culture is growing but less developed than El Salvador's established scene.

### Climate and Seasons

**Dry Season (November-April):** Your late November/early December ride falls in the dry season. This means:
- Minimal rainfall (mostly sunny days)
- Hot temperatures in coastal lowlands (28-35°C)
- Lower humidity than wet season (but still tropical)
- Best conditions for cycling and beach activities
- Morning offshore winds good for surfing

**Wet Season (May-October):**  
Afternoon thunderstorms, higher humidity, bigger surf swells. Roads can flood in heavy rains.

The dry season is optimal for this tour section, offering the best combination of weather conditions for cycling and beach activities.

## Historical Note: Pedro de Alvarado Connection

The border town Ciudad Pedro de Alvarado creates an interesting historical connection for your tour. Pedro de Alvarado (1485-1541) was the Spanish conquistador who led the conquest of Guatemala in 1524. He founded Santiago de los Caballeros de Guatemala (later moved and becoming Antigua after the 1773 earthquake).

Alvarado used this coastal location to build ships, which he sailed to Peru to join Francisco Pizarro's conquest of the Inca Empire. He later returned to Guatemala and died in 1541 during an expedition in Mexico.

You're leaving the city Alvarado founded (Antigua), passing through the coastal town named after him, to reach El Salvador - a fitting geographical narrative of the colonial Spanish expansion along the Pacific coast.

Exactly 506 years before your journey in November 2025, in November 1519, Hernán Cortés was beginning his march inland from the Gulf Coast toward Tenochtitlan (Mexico City). While Alvarado came slightly later to Guatemala (1524), this entire region shares the complex legacy of the Spanish conquest, indigenous resistance and adaptation, and the formation of modern Central American nations.

## The 2025 Bicycle Touring Perspective

### Why This Section Matters

Stages 110-111 represent a classic Central American bike touring experience:

**Geographic Drama:** Descent from colonial highlands through volcanic landscapes to tropical coast - 2,500m elevation change experiencing multiple climate zones in one day.

**Cultural Transition:** Colonial Spanish heritage → agricultural lowlands → international border → surf town modernity. You'll experience the full spectrum of Central American life.

**International Border:** Crossing from Guatemala to El Salvador by bicycle provides adventure and achievement that flying misses. It's a tangible transition between countries and cultures.

**Destination Appeal:** El Tunco offers perfect rest day activities - active recovery through surfing, beach relaxation, excellent food scene, and social surf town atmosphere.

**Historical Resonance:** Following ancient Spanish colonial routes and shipping corridors, modern highways built on centuries of connectivity between highlands and coast.

### What Makes It Special for NAE 2025

For full-tour riders, this section represents leaving the Mexico/Guatemala portion of the tour and entering the Central American "middle section" of El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua. It's a significant milestone.

For sectional riders like yourself covering Stages 83-128 (Puerto Vallarta to Panama City), this represents your first international border crossing and entry into Central American cultural zones distinct from Mexico and highland Guatemala.

The dramatic elevation loss, tropical heat, and arrival at the coast create memorable contrasts with earlier tour sections. After the climbing in Mexico and Guatemala, the coastal riding offers different challenges and rewards.

## Weather Forecast (Late November/Early December)

**Antigua Start:** Cool mornings (12-15°C), warming to 20-24°C by midday. Possibility of afternoon clouds. Mild and pleasant - enjoy it before descending!

**Coastal Plain & El Tunco:** Hot (28-35°C), humid, sunny. Minimal rain chance (dry season). Morning offshore winds. Evening sea breeze. This is tropical beach weather.

**Wind:** Generally light morning winds increasing by afternoon. Offshore morning winds good for surfing.

**Ocean Temp:** 26-29°C (no wetsuit needed)

## Final Thoughts: Antigua to El Tunco

This two-stage, 262km journey compresses remarkable geographic, climatic, and cultural transitions into an unforgettable bicycle touring experience. You'll begin at 1,500m in one of the Americas' finest colonial cities, surrounded by active volcanoes. By day's end, you'll be at sea level, having crossed an international border, in a surf town on the Pacific. 

The dramatic elevation loss of Stage 110, the adventure of the international border crossing, and the reward of arriving at El Tunco create a perfect touring narrative arc. Add in the cultural transition from Spanish colonial highlands to tropical surf town, and you have a section that epitomizes what makes bicycle touring in Central America so compelling.

Your rest day in El Tunco offers the perfect balance of recovery and engagement - whether you're taking surf lessons, lounging on the beach, or exploring the vibrant food and nightlife scene, you'll have earned this break while positioned perfectly to continue south toward Nicaragua and Costa Rica.

The route follows historic paths of Spanish colonial expansion, indigenous trade routes, and modern transportation corridors, all while showcasing the natural drama of the Central American volcanic arc meeting the Pacific Ocean. This is bicycle touring at its finest: challenging, rewarding, culturally rich, and scenically spectacular.
